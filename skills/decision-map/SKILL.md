# DecisionMap

Turn a messy strategic choice into a comparable option map and a testable working hypothesis. Preserve human decision ownership throughout.

## 1. Check scope

Proceed for business, product, market, competitive response, positioning, go-to-market, or marketing strategy decisions.

Stop and state the boundary for:

- military or political conflict
- legal or medical advice
- financial investment decisions
- mergers and acquisitions
- layoffs or HR restructuring

Confirm that there is a real choice between plausible paths, an identifiable decision owner, and a concrete objective. Rewrite the decision in one sentence before analysis.

## 2. Build the evidence frame

Separate every important claim into one of four labels:

- **Fact**: supported by supplied or cited evidence
- **Assumption**: believed but not established
- **Interpretation**: a reading of available evidence
- **Unknown**: material information that is missing

Never invent facts or conceal assumptions in confident prose. If current external evidence is necessary, research it only when tools and authorization allow. Cite the evidence used.

Capture the decision owner and objective, deadline or decision pressure, affected parties, available resources, hard constraints, evidence, and major unknowns.

Ask only questions whose answers could change the feasible options, risk, objective fit, or unsafe assumptions. If the remaining gaps do not block useful work, continue with a provisional map and mark confidence `Low`.

## 3. Create the strategy map

Generate 3–7 strategically distinct options. Do not create cosmetic variations of one idea.

For every option include:

1. Name and short summary
2. Expected upside
3. Price or cost across money, time, reputation, opportunity cost, operational complexity, customer trust, and strategic flexibility
4. Required resources and their availability
5. Key risks, early warning signals, and possible mitigations
6. Likely reactions from customers, competitors, partners, and internal stakeholders
7. Short-, medium-, and long-term effects
8. Core assumptions about the user, market, and other parties
9. Breakpoints that make the option unattractive or invalid
10. Signals to monitor
11. Confidence: `Low`, `Medium`, or `High`, with a short rationale

After the options, state the key trade-offs, dominant uncertainties, a shortlist of 1–3 options, the best current working hypothesis, and what evidence would change that view.

Use only `Low`, `Medium`, or `High` for strategic confidence. Do not use numeric or hybrid confidence scores.

## 4. Pressure-test the shortlist

For each shortlisted option examine:

- strategic logic and what must be true
- execution path and resource fit
- likely failure modes and invalidators
- smallest useful experiments
- reversibility and learning value
- updated confidence with rationale

Prefer tests that reduce a dominant uncertainty before creating large irreversible commitments.

## 5. Produce the decision summary

Present the result as a working strategic hypothesis, not a final truth. Include the selected path, accepted trade-offs, rejected or deferred options, immediate actions, signals, thresholds, revisit triggers, what would change the recommendation, and overall confidence with rationale.

The human decision owner makes the final choice.

## 6. Maintain an ongoing decision

When the decision evolves, append a cascade update rather than rewriting history. Record what changed, new facts, invalidated assumptions, signal movement, observed outcomes, next actions, and the next review trigger. Keep earlier versions visible so changes in judgment remain traceable.

## Output style

Use compact headings and comparison tables where they improve scanability. Mark uncertainty directly. Keep facts, assumptions, interpretations, and unknowns visibly separate. Avoid generic advice, false precision, and premature certainty.
